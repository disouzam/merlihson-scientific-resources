vCache: Verified Semantic Prompt Caching

Semantic caching is a mechanism that returns a previously saved answer even for a request (prompt) that was not formulated in exactly the same words, but is semantically close enough to it. By doing so, it saves repeated model runs, reduces latency, and significantly lowers computation costs.

Have you ever wondered why "Semantic Caching" mechanisms struggle to provide reliable and consistent results in production environments? After all, the idea of saving time and costs by recycling answers to similar queries sounds ideal. In today's review, we will dive into a paper that points out a systemic flaw in existing methods: the reliance on a global and uniform similarity threshold.

Meet vCache, an innovative solution that proposes abandoning the "uniform threshold" in favor of a personalized learning mechanism for each request, complete with a mathematical guarantee to control the error rate. Here is everything you need to know about the solution, its advantages, and the "fine print" behind the guarantee.

The problem is that existing solutions mostly work with a fixed similarity threshold. On paper, the idea is simple: if a new request is similar enough to a request already in the cache, the old answer is returned. In practice, such a global threshold is too blunt an instrument. If set too high, good "cache hits" are missed; if set too low, the system starts returning incorrect answers. Exactly at this point, vCache enters the picture, attempting to replace the fixed threshold with a more tailored and dynamic decision-making mechanism. The results report a dramatic improvement: up to 12.5 times more cache hits and up to 26 times fewer errors compared to baseline models.

Figure 3 in the paper says it all: the average similarity level for a correct cache hit stands at 0.85, while for an incorrect cache hit it stands at 0.84. In other words, the distributions are almost completely identical, and therefore no single global threshold can reliably separate them. An even stronger point emerges from the bottom part of that same chart: the optimal threshold varies across different embeddings, ranging broadly between 0.71 and 1.0. The problem is not just that the global threshold is inaccurate, but that the structure of the data inherently does not allow a single threshold to solve the problem reliably.

Instead of setting one threshold for all, vCache learns a dedicated threshold for each embedding in the cache. Furthermore, instead of making a rigid, binary "Hit or Miss" decision, the system learns the probability that the answer from the cache will be correct for a given similarity level. The user defines in advance the maximum error rate they are willing to tolerate (δ), and the system guarantees to meet it.

The paper's authors model the relationship between the similarity level and the correctness of the answer using a sigmoid function, ranging between 0 and 1, with only two parameters: t (the transition point) and γ (the sharpness of the transition). The choice of a sigmoid is not solely due to computational convenience; they empirically demonstrate on their data (Figures 7-8) that the true curve indeed looks approximately S-shaped.

The authors propose training a statistical model for each answer in the cache, which predicts the probability that it will be correct based on past experience. Since there are usually not enough past examples for this model to be sufficiently accurate, the paper chooses not to rely on it as is. Instead, it looks at the worst and most conservative scenario from a statistical safety range (a Confidence Band, which is an extension of a confidence interval to the curve representing the model). From this scenario, τ is calculated, which is the percentage of times we are obligated to "explore" and verify the answer (meaning, not relying solely on the cache) to ensure we do not exceed the permitted error threshold we defined.

To do this, the authors generate a random number between 0 and 1 every time a new request arrives. If the drawn number is less than or equal to τ, the system chooses "exploration," meaning it ignores the cache and sends the request to the model to get a new answer. If the number is greater than τ, the system chooses "exploitation" and uses the answer already saved in the cache, thereby saving time and resources without compromising overall reliability.

A few caveats: The paper has a real mathematical proof; Theorem 4.1 proves that if two assumptions hold, the error remains below δ. But the word "verified" in the title is a bit of a marketing term. The first assumption (that every new prompt is independent and identically distributed) breaks down in any multi-turn conversation, because the previous context affects the subsequent ones. The second assumption, that the relationship between similarity and correctness is sigmoidal, does not have to hold; it can be noisy, non-monotonic, or dependent on the task type. There is also a question about the benchmarks themselves: some of the prompts in SemCacheLMArena were synthetically generated by GPT-nano, meaning they are inherently "cleaner" than what exists in real production. This is a guarantee within their framework, not a real-world guarantee.

vCache did not solve semantic caching once and for all. It did something more important: it realized that the real problem is the calibration of the cache-hit decision, not the improvement of the embedding. It turns semantic cache from something "intuitive but dangerous" into something controllable and reasonable for production. And that is already a fine achievement.
