Review 599: Fixing the RL Diversity Illusion: Let's break some intuitions
Mike's daily paper review: 07.04.26, review 599
KL-REGULARIZED REINFORCEMENT LEARNING IS DESIGNED TO MODE COLLAPSE

When we fine-tune language models, we want them to learn to "behave according to our expectations" without losing their (relatively) natural diversity of answers. The prevailing opinion holds that the manner of training regularization (referring to training with RL) determines whether the model will explore many options or fixate on just one (which is good and aligned with our expectations). Specifically, it is commonly assumed that optimizing reverse KL divergence forces the model to seek a single mode (mode seeking), while forward KL encourages it to cover all possibilities (mass covering). This paper argues that for modern language models with very high expressiveness, this intuition is completely wrong. The authors prove mathematically (one of the reasons I liked the paper) that the standard regularized (introducing a new word to the lexicon) reward objective function is structurally built in a way that destroys diversity, and they propose an incredibly lightweight trick to save it.

Unsurprisingly, the core research object of the paper is the optimal target distribution of the model undergoing fine-tuning. When we run regularized RL training, we mathematically define a perfect target behavior that balances receiving high rewards with staying close to the base model. The inputs are simply this base model and a reward function, and the output is this ideal theoretical distribution, which is controlled by a single regularization coefficient.

To understand why diversity disappears, the authors examine the exact probability ratio between any two potential answers under this ideal target distribution. By calculating this ratio, they map out how the gap in rewards and the probabilities of the base model dictate the final outcome and find a "built-in flaw". If two answers earn exactly the same high reward, the training forces their final probabilities to perfectly reflect what the base model originally thought. It completely fails to promote rare but correct answers. Lowering the regularization strength only worsens the situation, excessively concentrating the model's focus solely on the most common answer.

To cope with this, they introduce Mode Anchored Reward Augmentation. The fix operates entirely within a training batch (that's "batch" in Hebrew, right?). The user defines a threshold separating good answers from bad ones. Afterwards, the algorithm finds within the batch the good answer with the highest probability (from the base model) to serve as an anchor. For every other good answer, the method artificially increases its reward based on the gap between its initial base probability and the anchor. This forces the mathematical objective function to value all good answers equally.

Standard approaches try to fix diversity collapse (mode collapse) by blindly altering the regularization strength, swapping the type of distance between distributions, or adding external diversity penalties using separate classification models. This work completely rejects the use of external signals. Instead of fighting the optimization with a separate penalty, the authors structurally rewrite the objective function. By injecting the probability gaps of the base model directly into the reward, they explicitly change the base target from a single peak to a wide plateau. This ensures the mathematics will treat all high-quality outputs with high and uniform probability density, instead of simply rubber-stamping the original biases of the base model.

This technique relies heavily on the quality threshold, which acts as a hard threshold just like a regularization coefficient. This rigid boundary assumes that every sample above the line is equally perfect, and completely throws away any signal of reward relationships among the very best outputs. Additionally, the method assumes (my own thought) that the optimizer is indeed capable of navigating the complex training surface to reach this global optimum, a rather difficult promise to fulfill when training LLMs. Finally, the theoretical proofs assume that the ideal distribution never tries to place mass on answers that the base model considers impossible. If an excellent answer has a probability of absolute zero under the base model, the method does not work.

The computational cost of this fix is practically zero. Calculating the augmented reward requires only a simple calculation inside. There is no need for additional forward passes or external model checks, which adds completely negligible time complexity to the standard training procedure.
