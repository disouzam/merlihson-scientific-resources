THE INFORMATION GEOMETRY OF SOFTMAX: PROBING AND STEERING

Continuing the line of reviews with somewhat "tough" mathematics regarding LLMs. This time, the paper discusses the subject of "concept shifting" within the representation space of our models. For example, if you want the model to mention a "Czech beer" every time we talk about any kind of beer, we can shift the model's weights in the direction of the "Czech beer" vector. The question is how to do this correctly so that instead of beer, we don’t end up with some "other Czech drink" (assuming one exists).

A new paper from the University of Chicago and INSEAD challenges the accepted assumption that the representation spaces of AI models possess a "flat Euclidean" structure. This began over 10 years ago with the word2vec paper, which claimed that representations of concepts could be composed linearly. You likely remember the ״king - man + woman = queen״ examples that became very famous at the time.

Instead, the authors argue that for models producing softmax distributions (from which tokens are sampled), the natural geometry is Information Geometry (there is an entire mathematical field dealing with this, by the way). In this approach, the distance between two representation vectors is defined by the difference in the probability distributions they generate (related to Bregman divergence, but there’s no need to dive that deep), rather than by the straight-line distance between them in space. The central object in this study is the representation vector used as input to the softmax layer, and the goal is to model how changes in these vectors affect the resulting categorical output distribution.

The authors define the representation space as a statistical manifold, a surface where each point corresponds to a specific probability distribution. To measure distance on this manifold, they use the Fisher Information Metric, a measure that quantifies the change in the output distribution in response to infinitesimal (extremely small) shifts in the representation vector. This metric weights different directions in the vector space based on the amount of "work" the model exerts to change its output in those directions.

The primary contribution is an algorithm called Dual Steering, which provides a method for modifying internal representations to change a specific concept (such as sentiment or a visual category) while preserving other unrelated information. The process begins with linear probing—a simple classifier trained to identify a target concept from the model's internal vectors. In standard steering, it is customary to move the representation directly along the direction of this probe. However, Dual Steering treats the probe direction as a covector, a mathematical object living in a dual space that explains how to measure a concept rather than how to move toward it. To actually shift the representation, the algorithm maps this covector back to the representation space using the inverse of the Fisher Information Metric. This transformation, known as a natural gradient, ensures that the steering step is performed in the direction that most efficiently increases the probability of the target concept relative to the model's internal geometry.

Technically, the method relies on the assumption that the local curvature of the representation space (the Fisher metric) can be captured and that the linear representation hypothesis holds locally. A key decision is the use of the Fisher metric as a preconditioner, which accounts for the fact that certain directions in the representation space may be saturated or highly sensitive depending on the model's weights. Unlike standard linear steering (the closest baseline), which treats all directions as equivalent, Dual Steering recognizes that the importance of a vector's direction depends on the local density of the softmax distribution.

An implicit failure mode of this method is its dependence on the accuracy of the local metric estimation; if the representation is in a region where the softmax is at an extreme peak or near zero, the Fisher metric may become unstable, leading to erratic steering steps. In terms of complexity, the method requires calculating or approximating the Fisher Information Matrix. For a representation space of dimension N, the full matrix has N^2 elements, making naive measurement quadratic relative to the representation size. However, since the metric is defined by the softmax weights, it can often be factorized or approximated to maintain linear scalability relative to the number of output categories or the representation dimension.
