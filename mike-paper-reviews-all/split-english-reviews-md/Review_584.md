Review 584: Beyond Direct Attention: Analyzing the Attention Mechanism as Markov Chains
Mike’s Daily Paper Review: 28.02.26
ATTENTION (AS DISCRETE-TIME MARKOV) CHAINS

Today’s review is relatively light compared to previous ones: aside from discrete-time Markov chains, no complex mathematical concepts are mentioned here. Nevertheless, the paper is non-trivial and, in my opinion, not particularly well-written. I am not certain if the proposed method will work, but conceptually, it is quite interesting. Note that the paper focuses primarily on the visual domain, where each token represents an image patch.

The authors propose a new interpretation of the self-attention mechanism by treating the attention weight matrix as a transition probability matrix within a discrete-time Markov chain (similar to a random walk). The authors suggest viewing each (visual) token as a state representative, where the attention score between two tokens defines the probability of transitioning from one state (token) to another. A discrete-time Markov chain is a mathematical model describing a sequence of possible transitions between tokens, where the probability of each transition depends solely on the previous state. Note that the attention matrix is a right-stochastic matrix, a matrix composed of non-negative numbers where the sum of each row is 1, representing a valid probability distribution over transitions to all possible subsequent states (tokens).

A key concept in the paper, Attention Chains, shifts the focus from immediate attention effects to a form of higher-order attention propagation. This is achieved through standard operations, such as selecting a token to see "what a single token attends to," starting from a 1-hot vector: a vector with a value of 1 at a specific token and 0 elsewhere.

The authors then take the attention scores and construct a Markov process over them, arguing that this may reveal indirect (aka multi-hop) relationships between visual tokens. The paper employs the power method, a technique that repeatedly multiplies a state vector by the transition matrix to observe how the attention distribution evolves over several steps. This allows the system to account for indirect (multi-stage) attention paths, where a token is considered important not just for its direct connections, but for its role in the deeper "flow" of information.

As these iterations progress, the distribution tends to accumulate in regions the authors identify as metastable states (sets). A metastable set of tokens is a subset where the probability of remaining within it is significantly higher than the probability of exiting its boundaries. This creates clusters or "pockets" of local attention that correspond to semantically coherent regions (patches), according to the authors' tests. Note that if the resulting Markov chain is aperiodic and irreducible (i.e., ergodic), the power method would not be strictly necessary, as the Markov chain derived from the attention scores would converge to a stationary distribution equal to the eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue 1. However, the paper claims this is not the case here.

To ensure this process converges to a unique solution, the method adopts an adjustment styled after PageRank, Google’s legendary website ranking algorithm: mixing the attention matrix with a small amount of uniform "token teleportation" probability (a random transition between any tokens). In effect, this makes every state (token) accessible from every other token and aperiodic, meaning the system does not return to a specific subset of tokens only at fixed time intervals (steps). It becomes aperiodic, but not necessarily irreducible.

The final output of this process (the jumps) is TokenRank, defined as the steady-state distribution vector of the Markov chain. This probability distribution remains unchanged under the transition operation and represents the global equilibrium of attention flow across all tokens. While standard attention mechanisms describe only first-order direct connections, TokenRank is designed to measure global importance by aggregating the influence of all possible paths through the chain. Note that this distribution depends on the starting state—i.e., a semantic region (such as an object) in the image. Essentially, these distribution vectors delineate object boundaries, performing a type of segmentation or object detection.

Furthermore, the paper proposes a weighting scheme for multi-head attention based on the second-largest eigenvalue of the transition matrix (derived from the attention weights). The second-largest eigenvalue in a transition matrix determines the mixing time, which is the number of transitions required to reach its steady state. A larger second eigenvalue indicates the presence of more stable metastable states, suggesting the head contains more structured information than random noise. The authors use these values to calculate a weighted average of the heads, prioritizing those with slower convergence rates to capture more distinct features.

The method relies on the assumption of square attention matrices, which limits its direct application to self-attention or cases where the sequence lengths of Q and K are identical. Cross-attention matrices represent a failure case, as they are typically rectangular and contain inaccessible states, preventing the construction of a standard Markov chain. The paper argues that this could be resolved by introducing dummy states with uniform transition probabilities; however, it does not detail the exact parameters for such an implementation. Regarding complexity, while calculating the second eigenvalue is noted as resource-intensive, the iterations for TokenRank are efficient, typically converging within 10 to 20 steps.
