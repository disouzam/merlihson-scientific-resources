# Mike’s Daily Paper Review: 07.12.25Gated Attention for Large Language Models: Non-linearity, Sparsity, and Attention-Sink-Free

Mike’s Daily Paper Review: 07.12.25Gated Attention for Large Language Models: Non-linearity, Sparsity, and Attention-Sink-Free

The standard multihead attention mechanism in the Transformer is, at its core, a "nearly linear sandwich" wrapped in a probability distribution. This sounds a bit unclear, but let's see what is inside: First, we project to V, mix them in a convex manner (with a vector normalized via Softmax), and project them once more with matrix W_O (after concatenating the outputs of all heads).

The authors of the paper argue that this architecture suffers from a low-rank bottleneck. Since the V projection and the W_O projection are essentially consecutive linear transformations operating on the weighted sum of tokens, the mechanism lacks inherent non-linearity between these stages. This structural limitation restricts the model's expressiveness and forces it to rely heavily on the Softmax bottleneck to filter information.

The innovation here is the introduction of Gated Attention, implemented specifically at the output of the Scaled Dot-Product Attention (or SDPA for short). While gating is common in FFNs and RNNs (such as LSTMs), its application directly onto the attention head output is the main novelty.

As mentioned, the method works by splitting the computation path after the attention operation but before the final aggregation. One path follows the standard Q-K-V generation and Softmax aggregation. The parallel path computes a "gating score." This score is derived via a learned linear projection of Q, which is then passed through a Sigmoid (which ensures it falls between 0 and 1).

The gating score is calculated with the Q representation and does not consider the value of V; it looks at the representation of the current token to decide how much of the retrieved content should actually pass to the residual stream (remember that in Transformers, we compute an addition to the representation from the previous layer, not the representation itself). The operation is element-wise multiplication (or Hadamard product in mathematical parlance) between the standard SDPA output and the gate vector which underwent sigmoid activation.

The authors investigate different variants of this gate computation: head-shared vs. head-specific, and element-wise vs. a single coefficient per attention head. The most effective method identified is Head-Specific Element-wise Gating. This means that every attention head has its own unique gating parameters that modulate each dimension of that head's output vector independently. This resolution allows the model to suppress specific token dimensions within a specific head while allowing others to pass.

Mathematically, this introduces two essential properties into the attention block.

First, it injects non-linearity directly into the (potentially) low-rank mapping between the V and W_O matrices. The composition of the V projection, the attention aggregation, and the O projection is no longer a simple sequence of linear transformations; it is now modulated by a data-dependent non-linear mask. This increases the rank and expressiveness of the transformation without significantly increasing the number of parameters.

Second, and perhaps more importantly, the sigmoid activation induces sparsity. Sigmoid functions naturally push values toward zero or one. This creates a situation where the model can actively zero out the output of an attention head if the retrieved information is deemed irrelevant to the current query context.

This gating mechanism fundamentally changes the dynamics of "Attention Sinks." In standard Transformers, the Softmax function must sum to one. If a token does not find relevant information in the context, it is forced to dump its probability mass somewhere, usually on the first token or a delimiter. This creates massive activation spikes and "sinks" that distort the hidden states.

The Gated Attention mechanism decouples information selection (Softmax) from information intensity (Gating). Even if the Softmax forces a high probability on a sink token due to normalization constraints, the sigmoid can recognize that the retrieval is irrelevant and push the output intensity to zero. This action effectively filters the noise generated by the Softmax normalization constraint before it contaminates the residual stream. The result is cleaner signal propagation, where the model is not forced to process massive and irrelevant activations from sink tokens, thereby stabilizing the internal feature distribution without the need for additional engineering of sink tokens.

Are LLMs Capable of Solving Multi-step Problems without Errors?

Roy and Mike’s Daily Article Review: 08.12.25

Solving a Million-Step LLM Task with Zero Errors

In the last year or two, it seems the focus, at least in the industry, has shifted to developing agents. In just the last few months, many companies have raised massive sums and reached seed records with the promise of training (or building) agents for tasks like security, coding, etc. While many use agents on a daily basis (Cursor / Claude Code), others continue to argue vigorously that due to the breakdown into steps and cumulative errors[1], what actually works better in practice are structured workflows.

The problem of cumulative errors was addressed as early as 1991 in the context of rounding errors in computation [2] and deep learning [3] (Bengio) and in other fields. Here, the authors suggest (following Anthropic) that the key is an error correction mechanism.

Using a beautiful analogy, they argue that complex systems involving many steps sensitive to errors usually have error correction mechanisms (DNA replication, error-correcting codes in hard drives and WiFi). They propose a sophisticated method to execute a large number of steps with zero errors (!).

The paper proposes an extreme decomposition of a problem into subtasks and exploits (quite beautifully) several properties of language models to create an error correction mechanism. The method as a whole is called Massively Decomposed Agentic Processes (MDAP), and the authors offer an implementation named MAKER. They demonstrate new scaling laws and a genuine advantage for a multi-agent system.

The Fundamental Problem The fundamental problem with using Large Language Models (LLMs) in long-horizon processes is that even if the error rate per step is 1%, the probability of making a mistake after 1,000 steps is practically 1.

In this paper, the authors chose to solve the Towers of Hanoi problem: involving three pegs and a number of disks, where the goal is to move all disks from the first to the third peg while strictly adhering to two rules: moving only one disk at a time and maintaining the condition that a larger disk is never placed on top of a smaller one. The number of steps in an optimal solution is 2D−1 (where D is the number of disks).

This problem has a simple programmatic solution, but models fail to solve it when D ≥ 6. Here, we are talking only about "general" models, not those trained specifically for the task. The Solution – Error Correction via:

1. Voting: At each step, the language model is queried repeatedly for the next move. The system counts how many "votes" each possible move receives until one option leads the second-place option by k votes (first-to-ahead-by-k).

2. Decomposition: Breaking the task down into the smallest possible steps (maximal decomposition).

3. Red-flagging:

    ◦ a. Correlated errors: We ask the model for two outputs (in this case, the move to be made and the state following the move). If there is a contradiction between them (or if we simply receive invalid output, like unbalanced brackets), we discard this output and do not use it for the vote count.

    ◦ b. Output too long: You know how models start getting tangled in loops? That. Similarly, we discard this output.

Diversity between outputs is achieved by setting the model's sampling temperature to 0.1. In the appendix, they indirectly show that by asking the model to provide an answer that must include (also) the next step and the post-step state, the model outputs quite a few tokens before answering, which also introduces significant diversity.

When k is large, this probability approaches 1.

What is the chance of getting one million correct steps using this mechanism? Given the probability p, we can choose a parameter k large enough so that such a mechanism will succeed even over a million steps. And if p approaches 1, the number of required calls decays.

All of this assumes a worst-case scenario where all votes that are not for the correct step go to a single competing alternative (meaning if p = 0.6, then 0.4 of the calls are votes for a specific incorrect solution). If the number of error possibilities is larger, one reaches a probability of 1 for a correct solution with fewer calls (or a smaller k).

Additionally, the authors develop a formula for estimating the minimal k (kmin​) required to solve the task with probability t, given p, total steps s, and the number of steps per call m.

They use this to calculate how much it would cost to solve the task, and then conduct an experiment comparing models—on a benchmark that no company has yet overfit (!). Each model has a token cost, a base probability of being correct (p), and an average number of tokens it outputs (in this task).

The most cost-effective model is gpt-4.1-mini, quite unsurprisingly. This happens even though the chance of being correct is much higher in o3-mini and there are models that cost much, much less.

Details on Correction Mechanisms: They nicely show the correlation between output length and the chance of being correct (and by the way, also between output length and the chance that the two outputs will contain a contradiction).

Finally, the authors get a bit carried away in a discussion about superintelligence, emphasize the importance of decorrelation of errors, and show a parallel to microservices architecture. Fair enough.

Critique: Towers of Hanoi is about the only problem that can be decomposed into steps like this; it doesn't represent much, and one can write code to solve it (in the third lesson of Intro to CS, if I recall correctly). In the appendix, they also show improvement on long multiplication (the problem must be decomposable into small steps).

Overall, a very beautiful idea. It takes the most annoying thing about models, their non-determinism (in practice),and leverages it to create a voting and error correction mechanism.

How can the same principles powering cellular communication lead to robust watermarks in language models?

Dor and Mike’s Daily Paper Review: Nov 14, 2025, Review #549, 475 reviews to go until 1024

HeavyWater and SimplexWater: Watermarking Low‑Entropy Text Distributions

The paper proposes a new theoretical framework for analyzing and developing watermarks for large language models (LLMs), introducing two new schemes within it: HeavyWater and SimplexWater. The paper focuses on one of the most difficult problems in this field: embedding watermarks in tasks where most of the probability mass is concentrated on a small portion of the vocabulary during generation, as typically happens in cases like code generation or solving mathematical equations. Such texts possess low entropy. In images, a watermark is embedded either on the image itself or in some latent space where "creation occurs." In contrast, in language models, most watermarks are embedded on the token distribution (i.e., on the logits).

The first step in the paper is creating a "shared language." The authors formulate an optimization problem describing the following scenario: assume there are two people trying to communicate the existence of a watermark—one embedding the watermark and the other trying to detect it. All they share is a secret key (some integer), which serves as a random seed on both their computers and allows both to sample the same values given some predetermined distribution. Additionally, they share a function that assigns a score to each token. The scoring function constitutes a sort of "shared language" via a rule of thumb: tokens that received a higher score will receive a higher probability after the distribution update, ensuring that at the detection stage, a sequence of high-scoring tokens will be effectively watermarked. The reason for the key's secrecy is to prevent hostile interference in the process—a third party aware of the algorithm and the key would be able to easily remove or steal the watermark from the text. In the paper, the authors focus on the trade-off between creating a watermark that is easy to detect on one hand, but does not harm the quality of the generated text on the other. Based on the mathematical model, the authors propose two new solutions to the problem, distinguished by the nature of the scoring function:

The first watermark (named SimplexWater) stems from a surprising connection to Coding Theory: the research field at the foundation of the digital communication world. Under the mathematical model the authors propose, one can view the scoring function as a collection of binary vectors. Under this view, the optimization problem transforms into a search for a collection of binary vectors with maximal Hamming distance. This is a classic problem from coding theory, with a variety of theoretical performance analyses and sophisticated algorithms. The equivalence that emerged opens a gate to "import" these results and the algorithms that have proven themselves for decades to solve a seemingly unrelated problem—embedding watermarks in a language model. The name of the watermark comes from a well-known algorithm from coding theory that the authors use, called Simplex Code.

The second watermark (named HeavyWater) comes from a different direction. The authors analyze the watermark created by researchers from OpenAI. The method relies on sampling from a distribution called Gumbel (the same one from the Gumbel trick) and deciding which tokens to sample according to the sampling result. The authors show that this method is essentially a special case of the model they built. What does this yield? The model helps understand what makes this watermark strong; it turns out that what determines the watermark's strength is the tail of the distribution—how fast the probability density decays at the limit. Consequently, by sampling the scoring function from a distribution with a heavier tail than Gumbel, the authors succeed in surpassing the performance of the popular method at a negligible cost.

The model the authors propose formalizes watermark embedding as an optimal-transport problem. Beyond the fact that this formulation allows them to use efficient algorithms to embed watermarks, the resulting watermarks do not harm text quality (on average, the token distribution does not change!).

The paper combines theory and practice. On one hand, the mathematical formulation and the use of fields like coding theory and optimal transport (for calculating distance between distributions) give the authors tools to prove optimality. On the other hand, the algorithms created in practice are very strong and beat the existing popular methods in the field.

Something we love about this work is that we succeeded in taking results developed by those greater and smarter than us over the last decades and applying them to quite relevant problems that interest the community.
